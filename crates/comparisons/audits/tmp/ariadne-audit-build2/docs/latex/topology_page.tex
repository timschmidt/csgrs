This page describes the topological foundations of the computable analysis described in the \doxysectlink{computable_analysis_page}{Foundations of Computable Analysis}{0} page. For details on how this is implemented in Ariadne, see the Topology\+Module documentation.\hypertarget{topology_page_computable_topological_spaces_section}{}\doxysubsection{\texorpdfstring{Computable Topological Spaces}{Computable Topological Spaces}}\label{topology_page_computable_topological_spaces_section}
As defined in Weihrauch (2000), a {\itshape computable topological space} consists of a topological space $(X,\tau)$ with countable basis $\beta$, with some partial surjective labelling function $nu:\Sigma^*\psfto \beta$. The {\itshape standard representation} $\delta$ of $X$ lists all words $w\in\dom(\nu)$ such that $x\in\nu(w)$.

For technical reasons, we require $\emptyset\in\beta$ and that $\dom(\nu)$ is recursively-\/enumerable (i.\+e. we can effectively list all elements of $\beta$). In practise, it is more convenient to consider the basic open sets $I\in\beta$, rather than their names $\nu^{-1}(I)$.

In order to perform standard operations of $X$ and its subsets, we require that intersection $J_1 \cap J_2 \neq \emptyset$ for $J_1,J_2\in\beta$ is verifiable. It is also useful to be able to test for subsets $I,\bar{I}\subset J_1\cup\cdots\cup J_n$.

In practise, it is not absolutely necessary to use open basic sets, and it is often more convenient to use compact sets as basic sets $\bar{\beta}$. However, this only works for locally-\/compact spaces, and we require that for every point $x$ and every open $U\ni x$, that there is a compact basic set $K \in \bar{\beta}$ such that $x\in K^\circ \subset U$.

Two main ways of constructing computable topological spaces are as {\itshape completions}, either the standard Cauchy completion of a metric space, for which {\itshape balls} are the basic sets, or as a completion of a partially-\/ordered set, for which basic sets are given by lower-\/ and upper-\/ {\itshape bounds}.\hypertarget{topology_page_metricspaces}{}\doxysubsection{\texorpdfstring{Metric spaces}{Metric spaces}}\label{topology_page_metricspaces}
We now show how to construct computable topological spaces from metric spaces. {\bfseries{References}} K. Weihrauch and U. Schreiber, "{}\+Embedding metric spaces into CPOs"{}, {\itshape Theoretical Computer Science} {\bfseries{16}}, pp5-\/24, 1981

A {\itshape metric} on a set $X$ is a function $d:X\times X\to \R^+$ satisfying\+:
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item Reflexivity\+: $d(x_1,x_2)=0\iff x_1=x_2$.
\item Symmetry\+: $d(x_1,x_2)=d(x_2,x_1)$.
\item Triangle inequality\+: $d(x_1,x_3)\leq d(x_1,x_2)+d(x_2,x_3)$.
\end{DoxyItemize}

A {\itshape metric space} is a pair $(X,d)$ where $d$ is a metric on $X$.

The topology of a metric space is generated by the balls $B(c,r):=\{ x\in X | \mid d(c,x) < r \}$. Then a sequence $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ converges to $x_\infty$ if $\forall\epsilon>0,\;\exists N\in\N,\; n\geq N \implies d(x_n,x_\infty)\leq\epsilon$.

A sequence $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ is a {\itshape Cauchy} sequence if $\forall\epsilon>0,\;\exists N\in\N,\; m,n\geq N \implies d(x_m,x_n)\leq\epsilon$. A metric space is {\itshape complete} if every Cauchy sequence converges.\hypertarget{topology_page_metriccompletion}{}\doxysubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Metric completion}{Metric completion}}\label{topology_page_metriccompletion}
Let $Q$ be a countable set, and $d:Q\times Q\to \R^+$ be a metric on $Q$. Then the {\itshape metric completion} $X=\overline{Q}$ of $Q$ consists of equivalence classes of {\itshape fast-\/converging Cauchy sequences} of the form $(q_0,q_1,q_2,\ldots)$ satisfying $d(q_m,q_n)\leq 2^{-\min(m,n)}$ for all $m,n$, under the equivalence relation $\seq{q}_1\sim\seq{q}_2\iff \forall n,\;d(q_{1,n},q_{2,n})\leq 2\!\times\!2^{-n}$.

Here it is important that the rate of convergence is known. An equivalent definition would be to specify a convergence rate $e_n$ for each sequence, with $d(q_m,q_n)\leq e_{\min(m,n)}$; the definition of fast convergence corresponds to $e_n=2^{-n}$.

Given that the $n^\textrm{th}$ element of the sequence is $q_n$, it must be the case that the limit $x$ lies in $B(q_n,2^{-n})$. Hence a base for the topology of $X$ is given by balls with centres in $Q$ and rational (or dyadic) radius.\hypertarget{topology_page_partial_order_lattice_section}{}\doxysubsection{\texorpdfstring{Partial Orders and Lattices}{Partial Orders and Lattices}}\label{topology_page_partial_order_lattice_section}
We now present elements of the theory of partial orders and lattices needed to construct representations by lower and/or upper bounds on elements.

{\bfseries{References}} Gierz, Hofmann, Keimel, Lawson, Mislove \& Scott, "{}\+A compendium of Continuous Lattices"{}, Springer, 1980.\hypertarget{topology_page_partial_order_subsection}{}\doxysubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Partial orders}{Partial orders}}\label{topology_page_partial_order_subsection}
A {\itshape partial order} on a set $X$ is a relation $\leq$ on $X\times X$ such that
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item Reflexivity\+: $\forall x\in X,\ x\leq x$.
\item Antisymmetry\+: $\forall x_1,x_2\in X,\ x_1\leq x_2\tand x_2\leq x_1 \timplies x_1=x_2$.
\item Transitivity\+: $\forall x_1,x_2,x_3\in X,\ x_1\leq x_2\tand x_2\leq x_3 \timplies x_1\leq x_3$.
\end{DoxyItemize}

A partial order is a {\itshape total order} if additionally
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item Connexity\+: $\forall x_1,x_2\in X, \ x_1\leq x_2 \tor x_2\leq x_1 $.
\end{DoxyItemize}

We write $x_1<x_2$ if $x_1\leq x_2$ and $x_2\not\leq x_1$, noting that the condition $x_2\not\leq x_1$ can be equivalently replaced by $x_1\neq x_2$. If $\leq$ is a total order, then $x_1 < x_2 \iff x_2 \not\leq x_1$.\hypertarget{topology_page_upperlowerbounds}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Upper and lower bounds}{Upper and lower bounds}}\label{topology_page_upperlowerbounds}
An element $u\in X$ is an {\itshape upper bound} for $A\subset X$ if $\forall a\in A,\;a\leq u$. Similarly, a {\itshape lower bound} $l$ for $A$ satisfied $\forall a\in A,\;l\leq a$.

A set $A\subset X$ is {\itshape upper/lower-\/bounded} if it has an upper/lower bound, and {\itshape bounded} if it has both an upper and a lower bound.

An element $s\in X$ is the {\itshape least upper bound} or {\itshape supremum} for $A\subset X$, denoted $\sup{A}$ or $\bigwedge A$, if $s$ is an upper bound, and $s\leq u$ for all upper bounds of $A$. The supremum of $A$ need not exist, but if it does, then it is unique. ~\newline
 Similarly, the {\itshape infemum} of $A$, denoted $\inf{A}$ or $\bigwedge A$, is the greatest lower bound, if it exists. ~\newline
 Symbolically, the supremum and infemum are defined\+: \[ s=\sup{A} \iff (\forall a\in A,\;a\leq s) \wedge \bigl(\forall u\in X,\;(\forall a\in A,\,a\leq u) \Rightarrow s\leq u\bigr) . \] \[ r=\inf{A} \iff (\forall a\in A,\;r\leq a) \wedge \bigl(\forall l\in X,\;(\forall a\in A,\,l\leq a) \Rightarrow l\leq r\bigr) . \]\hypertarget{topology_page_upperlowersets}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Upper and lower sets}{Upper and lower sets}}\label{topology_page_upperlowersets}
A subset $U$ of $X$ is an {\itshape upper set} or {\itshape upwardly closed set} if $\forall u\in U,\;\forall x\in X,\ u\leq x \implies x\in U$. A {\itshape lower set} $L$ is defined similarly; $\forall l\in L,\;\forall x\in X,\ x\leq l \implies x\in L$. For any subset $A$ of $X$, define $\uparrow\!A=\{x\in X \mid  \exists\,a\in A,\;a\leq x\}$ and $A^u=\{x\in X \mid  \forall a\in A,\;a\leq x\}$; similarly $\downarrow\!A=\{x\in X \mid  \exists\,a\in A,\;x\leq a\}$ and $S_l=\{x\in X \mid  \forall a\in A,\;x\leq a\}$. Note that $\uparrow\!{A}$ is the smallest upper set containing $A$, and $A^u$ is the upper set consisting of all upper-\/bounds for $A$.\hypertarget{topology_page_directedsets}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Directed sets}{Directed sets}}\label{topology_page_directedsets}
A subset $S$ of $X$ is an {\itshape upward directed set} if $\forall s_1,s_2\in S,\,\exists t\in S,\;s_1\leq t\tand s_2\leq t$. A {\itshape downward directed set} is defined similarly as $\forall s_1,s_2\in S,\,\exists t\in S,\;t\leq s_1\tand t\leq s_2$.\hypertarget{topology_page_partialorderexamples}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Examples of partial orders}{Examples of partial orders}}\label{topology_page_partialorderexamples}
We shall typically see (partial) orders in two contexts. The first is in fundamental types, like $\Q$ and $\R$, with a natural order relation. In this case, we are interested in using the order to define a natural basis for computation. The second is in information about values, where $\hat{x}$ gives information about an exact value $x$. In this case, we define $\hat{x}_1\leq \hat{x}_2 \iff \hat{x}_1 \supset \hat{x}_2$, so larger elements provide "{}more"{} information.

{\itshape Example} The standard ordering $\leq$ is a total order on the rational numbers $\Q$.

{\itshape Example} Given partially-\/ordered sets $X,Y$, there is a natural partial order on the product $X\times Y$ given by $(x_1,y_1)\leq(x_2,y_2) :\!\iff x_1\leq x_2 \tand y_1\leq y_2$.

{\itshape Example} Define the {\itshape information order} on open subsets of $X$ by $U_1\leq U_2 :\!\iff U_1 \supset U_2$. In other words, the information provided by the knowledge that $x\in U_2$ is more than that provided by $x\in U_1$. ~\newline
 A {\itshape filter} is a downward-\/closed upper-\/directed set of nonempty opens. i.\+e. a set $ \mathcal{U} $ such that $ U\in\mathcal{U}\tand U\subset V \timplies V\in\mathcal{U}$, and $ U_1,U_2\in\mathcal{U} \timplies \exists V\in\mathcal{U},\;U_1\cap U_2\supset V$. In particular, we see that $U_1,U_2\in\mathcal{U}\implies U_1\cap U_2\in \mathcal{U}$. If $X$ is a Hausdorff space, then any point $x$ defines a maximal filter by $\{U\in\mathcal{O}(X) \mid x\in U\}$.\hypertarget{topology_page_lattice_subsection}{}\doxysubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Lattices}{Lattices}}\label{topology_page_lattice_subsection}
A {\itshape join-\/lattice} is a set $X$ with a binary operation $\vee:X\times X\to X$ satisfying\+:
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item Commutativity\+: $x_1\vee x_2 = x_2\vee x_1$
\item Associativity\+: $(x_1\vee x_2)\vee x_3 = x_1 \vee (x_2\vee x_3)$
\item Idempotency\+: $x\vee x=x$.
\end{DoxyItemize}

Given a join-\/lattice $(X,\vee)$, we can define a natural partial order by $x_1\leq x_2 :\!\iff x_1\vee x_2=x_2$. ~\newline
 A partially ordered set $(X,\leq)$ has a natural join-\/lattice structure if every pair of elements $x_1,x_2$ has a least upper-\/bound, with $x_1\vee x_2 := \sup\{x_1,x_2\}$.

Similarly, a {\itshape meet-\/lattice} has a binary operation $\wedge$ satisfying commutativity, associativity and idempotency. ~\newline
 However, given a meet-\/lattice, we define the natural partial order by $x_1\leq x_2 :\!\iff x_1=x_1\wedge x_2$. ~\newline
 Given a partial order $\leq$, we define the natural meet-\/lattice structure assuming every pair of elements has a greatest lower-\/bound by $x_1\wedge x_2 := \inf\{x_1,x_2\}$.

A {\itshape lattice} is a set $X$ with binary operations {\itshape meet} $\wedge$ and {\itshape join} $\vee$, each of which is commutative and associative, further satisfying\+:
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item Absorption\+: $x_1 \vee (x_1\wedge x_2)=x_1 = x_1 \wedge (x_1\vee x_2)$
\end{DoxyItemize}

Since $x \vee x = x \vee \bigl(x \wedge (x \vee x)\bigr) = x$ and similarly $x\wedge x = x \wedge \bigl(x \vee (x \wedge x)\bigr) = x$, both $\vee$ and $\wedge$ are idempotent.

From the absorption property, we see that $x_1=x_1\wedge x_2 \iff x_1\vee x_2 = x_2$, so the partial order obtained from the join-\/lattice structure is the same as that obtained from the meet-\/lattice structure.

A lattice is {\itshape distributive} if $x_1\vee (x_2\wedge x_3) = (x_1\wedge x_2) \vee (x_1\wedge x_3)$, or equivalently, $x_1\wedge (x_2\vee x_3) = (x_1\vee x_2) \wedge (x_1\vee x_3)$.\hypertarget{topology_page_lattice_examples}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Examples of lattices}{Examples of lattices}}\label{topology_page_lattice_examples}
{\itshape Example} Any totally-\/ordered set $(X,\leq)$ is a lattice, with meet $x_1\wedge x_2 := \min(x_1,x_2)=\bigl[\text{if }x_1\leq x_2 \text{ then } x_1 \text{ else } x_2\bigr]$ and join $x_1\vee x_2=\max(x_1,x_2)$.

{\itshape Example} Since $\leq$ is a total order on the rationals, $\Q$ is a lattice. Note that the set $\{q\in \Q \mid q< 0 \vee q^2< 2\}$ does not have a supremum in $\Q$.

{\itshape Example} If $X,Y$ are lattices, then so is $X\times Y$, with $(x_1,y_1)\vee(x_2,y_2)=(x_1\vee x_2,y_1\vee y_2)$ and $(x_1,y_1)\wedge(x_2,y_2)=(x_1\wedge x_2,y_1\wedge y_2)$.\hypertarget{topology_page_complete_lattice_subsection}{}\doxysubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Complete partial orders / lattices}{Complete partial orders / lattices}}\label{topology_page_complete_lattice_subsection}
A partially-\/ordered set $(X,\leq)$ is {\itshape (countably) complete lattice} if every (countable) subset $A$ has a least-\/upper-\/bound (supremum) $\bigvee\!A$ and a greatest-\/lower-\/bound (infemum) $\bigwedge\!A$.

Note that if either of $\bigvee$ or $\bigwedge$ is defined, then so is the other. For if $\bigvee$ is defined, we can take $\bigwedge A = \bigvee\{x\in X \mid \forall a\in A,\,x\leq a\}$. Similarly, $\bigvee A = \bigwedge\{x\in X \mid \forall a\in A,\,a\leq x\}$.

We say $(X,\leq)$ is {\itshape (countably) upward directed-\/complete} if every (countable) upward directed subset $A$ has a supremum.

We say $(X,\leq)$ is {\itshape upper-\/bounded complete} if every upper-\/bounded set has a supremum, with similar definitions of lower-\/bounded complete and bounded-\/complete.\hypertarget{topology_page_ordercompletion}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Dedekind-\/\+Mac\+Neille order completion}{Dedekind-\/\+Mac\+Neille order completion}}\label{topology_page_ordercompletion}
A {\itshape completion} of a partially-\/ordered set $Q$ is an order-\/preserving embedding into a complete lattice $X$.

The {\itshape Dedekind-\/\+Mac\+Neille completion} $\overline{Q}$ of $Q$ consist of all pairs $(L,U)$ of subsets of $Q$ such that $L^u=U$ and $U_l=L$. It is the "{}smallest"{} possible complete lattice embedding $Q$.

If we only require {\itshape upper-\/bounded} sets to have a supremum, then we take $U$ nonempty; similarly for lower-\/bounded sets having an infemum. ~\newline
 It is clear that $\overline{Q}$ has a minimal subset which contains $Q$ and is closed under finite $\wedge$ and $\vee$, which is the natural algebraic closure of $Q$ as a lattice.

The partial order on $\overline{Q}$ is defined by $(L_1,U_1)\leq (L_2,U_2) \iff L_1 \subset L_2$, equivalently $U_1 \supset U_2$. ~\newline
 The meet operation is $(L_1,U_1)\wedge(L_2,U_2)=\bigl(L_1\cap L_2,(L_1\cap L_2)^u\bigr)$, and the join operation is $(L_1,U_1)\vee(L_2,U_2)=\bigl((U_1\cap U_2)_l,U_1\cap U_2\bigr)$ ~\newline
 The embedding of $Q$ into $\overline{Q}$ is defined by $q\mapsto(\{q\}_l,\{q\}^u)$. Clearly, $q_1\leq q_2$ if, and only if, $\{q_1\}_l \subset \{q_2\}_l$, so the embedding respects the partial order.

In computability theory, we shall be concerned with the case $Q$ is countable, in which case $\overline{Q}$ has at most continuum cardinalty, and the algebraic closure of $Q$ is again countable.\hypertarget{topology_page_orderrepresentations}{}\doxysubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Order representations}{Order representations}}\label{topology_page_orderrepresentations}
A natural way of representing elements of partially-\/ordered spaces is by countable directed sets. For simplicity, we counsider the the case of a complete lattice $X$ which is the completion of a countable lattice $Q$.

Any increasing sequence $q_0\leq q_1\leq\cdots$ maps into $\overline{Q}$ by taking $U=\{q_0,q_1,\ldots\}^u$ and $L=\bigl(\{q_0,q_1,\ldots\}^u\bigr)_l$. Similarly, any decreasing sequence $q_0\geq q_1\geq\cdots$ maps into $\overline{Q}$ by taking $L=\{q_0,q_1,\ldots\}_l$.

If $Q$ is a countable lattice, then any element of $\overline{Q}$ is the supremum of an increasing sequence, and the infemum of a decreasing sequence in $Q$. This gives two natural representations of $X=\overline{Q}$, the {\itshape lower representation} $\delta_<$ by increasing sequences in $Q$, and the {\itshape upper representation} $\delta_>$ by decreasing sequences. Giving both an increasing and a decreasing sequence converging to the same element yields the standard (bidirected) representation $\delta$. We write $\X_<$, $\X_>$ and $X$ (sometimes $\X_\lessgtr$) for the respective types.\hypertarget{topology_page_directedsetmonotonesequences}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Directed sets and monotone sequences}{Directed sets and monotone sequences}}\label{topology_page_directedsetmonotonesequences}
{\itshape Proposition} Let $Q$ be a lattice subset of a complete lattice $X$. If $A$ is any countable upward-\/directed subset of $Q$, then we can construct an increasing sequence $x_0\leq x_1\leq x_2\leq\cdots$ in $Q$ such that $\bigvee\!A = \bigvee_{n=0}^{\infty}x_n$. ~\newline
 For enumerating $A=\{a_0,a_1,\ldots\}$, we recursively define $(x_n)_{n\in\N}$ by $x_0=a_0$ and $x_{n+1}=a_{n+1}\vee x_{n}$. ~\newline
 Similarly, if $A$ is a countable downward-\/directed set, then we can construct a decreasing sequence $\cdots \leq x_2 \leq x_1 \leq x_0$ by taking $x_{n+1}=a_{n+1}\wedge x_{n}$.\hypertarget{topology_page_falsisficationoforder}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Falsification of ordering}{Falsification of ordering}}\label{topology_page_falsisficationoforder}
We have seen that we can extend $\leq$ over $X$. It is straightforward to see that $y \leq z$ if, and only if, whenever $y=\bigvee Y$ and $z=\bigwedge Z$, we have $\tilde{y}\leq\tilde{z}$ for all $\tilde{y}\in Y$ and $\tilde{z}\in Z$. This gives rise to an algorithm for falsifying $y\leq z$, or equivalently, verifying $y\not\leq z$ for $y:\X_<$ and $z:\X_>$, assuming $\leq$ is falisfiable on $Q\times Q$.

{\itshape Theorem} Suppose $\leq$ is falsifiable on $Q$; equivalently, $\not\leq$ is verifiable. Then the relation $\not\leq$ is verifiable on $\X_<\times \X_>$, with $\bigvee y_n \not \leq \bigwedge z_n \iff \exists n,\; y_n\not\leq z_n$. ~\newline
 {\itshape Proof} Let $y=\bigvee y_n$, $z=\bigwedge z_n$. If $y_m\leq z_n$ for all $m,n$, then $y\leq z_n$ for all $n$, so $y\leq z$. Hence if $y\not\leq z$, then there exists $m,n$ such that $y_m \not\leq z_n$, which can be verified. Conversely, if $y\leq z$, then for all $m,n$, $y_m\leq y\leq z\leq z_n$, so $y_m\leq z_n$.\hypertarget{topology_page_robusttotalorder}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Verification of total ordering}{Verification of total ordering}}\label{topology_page_robusttotalorder}
For a total order, $z\not\leq y \iff y < z \implies y\leq z$, it is natural to verify $y\leq z$ by considering the stronger property $y < z$, which is equivalent to falsifying $z\leq y$.\hypertarget{topology_page_robustpartialorder}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Robust verification of partial ordering}{Robust verification of partial ordering}}\label{topology_page_robustpartialorder}
We cannot expect the relation $y\leq z$ itself to be verifiable, since it includes the case $y=z$. For a partial order, the relation $y<z$ defined by $y\leq z$ and $y\neq z$ turns out to be too strong to be verified. However, given $y:\X_>$ and $z:\X_<$, we can find conditions under which $y\leq z$ is verifiable.

Suppose we want to verify $y\leq z$ for $y:\X_>$ and $z:\X_<$. Then given a description of $y$ as a sequence $y_n\searrow y$, and $z$ as a sequence $z_n\nearrow Y$, we need that $y_m\leq z_n$ for some $m,n$; equivalently $y_n\leq z_n$ for some $n$. However, the property of $y$ being "{}provably less"{} than $z$ should be {\itshape extensional}, so verification should be {\itshape independent} of the approximating sequences chosen. We therefore need a a stronger relation between elements of the sequence which ensures that the check is independent of the particular name used.

{\itshape Definition} Let $X$ be a complete lattice. Then the {\itshape (bidirectional) way-\/ordered} relation $\prec$ is defined by $y\prec z$ if whenever $Y$ is a downward-\/directed set with $y\geq\bigwedge Y$ and $Z$ upward-\/directed set with $z\leq\bigvee Z$, there exists $\tilde{y}\in Y,\,\tilde{z}\in Z$ with $\tilde{y}\leq\tilde{z}$.

It can easily be shown that the bidirectional way-\/ordered relation is an {\itshape (bidirectional) auxiliary order} satisfying\+:
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item $y\prec z \implies y\leq z$;
\item $u\leq y\prec z\leq v \implies u \prec v$;
\item $y_1\prec z \tand y_2\prec z \implies y_1\vee y_2\prec z$.
\item $y\prec z_1 \tand y\prec z_2 \implies y\prec z_1\wedge z_2$.
\end{DoxyItemize}

This way-\/ordered relation is similar to the "{}way-\/below"{} relation of Scott domain theory (see \doxysectlink{topology_page_scott_domain_section}{Scott Domain Theory}{2} for details).

{\itshape Definition} Let $Q$ be a sublattice of a complete lattice $X$. Then $Q$ is {\itshape approximating} if $\forall x\in X,\;x=\bigvee\{q\in Q\mid q\prec x\}=\bigwedge\{q\in Q\mid x\prec q\}$.

The following result shows that the way-\/below relation $\prec$ is an appropriate "{}robust"{} version of the partial order $\leq$ for verifying tests of the form $a\leq x$ for $a\in Q$ and $x\in X_<$.

{\itshape Theorem} The following are equivalent\+:
\begin{DoxyEnumerate}
\item $y\prec z$ i.\+e. whenever $y\geq\bigwedge Y$ and $z\leq\bigvee Z$, then $\exists \tilde{y}\in Y,\,\tilde{z}\in Z\;\tilde{y}\leq\tilde{z}$.
\item For all upper-\/directed sets $Y$ and a lower-\/directed sets $Z$ with $y=\bigwedge Y$ and $z=\bigvee Z$, then $\exists \tilde{y}\in Y,\tilde{z}\in Z,\;\tilde{y}\prec\tilde{z}$.
\item There exist an upper-\/directed set $Y$ and a lower-\/directed set $Z$ with $y=\bigwedge Y$, $z=\bigvee Z$, and $\exists \tilde{y}\in Y,\tilde{z}\in Z,\;\tilde{y}\prec\tilde{z}$.
\end{DoxyEnumerate}

{\itshape Corollary} Suppose $\prec$ is effectively verifiable on $Q$ and $Q$ is approximating for $X$. Then $\prec$ extends to a relation on $X \times X$ which is effectively verifiable on $\X_>\times \X_<$.

{\itshape Remark} If $X$ is a discrete space, then it may be the case that $x\prec x$. This shows that $x \prec y \not\Rightarrow y\not\leq x$. As a consequence, may use that {\itshape robut less-\/than} relation $x<y :\iff x\prec y\wedge y\not\leq x$ instead of simply $\prec$.\hypertarget{topology_page_ordertopologies}{}\doxysubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Order topologies}{Order topologies}}\label{topology_page_ordertopologies}
We now consider topologies on partially ordered sets.\hypertarget{topology_page_totallyorderedspace}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Totally ordered space}{Totally ordered space}}\label{topology_page_totallyorderedspace}
It is well-\/known that a total order $\leq$ on $X$ defines a natural topology such that for all $a,b\in X$, $\{x\in X\mid a < x \tand x < b\}$ is open. There are also natural topologies of {\itshape lower convergence}, with open sets $\{x\in X \mid a < x\}$ and of {\itshape upper convergence} with open sets $\{x\in X \mid x < a\}$.

If $X=\overline{Q}$, then a base for the topology is given by sets $\{x\in X\mid p < x \tand x < q\}$ for $p,q\in Q$.\hypertarget{topology_page_partialorderedspace}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Partially ordered space}{Partially ordered space}}\label{topology_page_partialorderedspace}
{\itshape Definition} A {\itshape partially-\/ordered space} is a topological space $(X,\tau)$ with a partial order $\leq$ which satisfies any of the following equivalent definitions\+:
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item The set $\{(x_1,x_2)\in X\times X\mid x_1\leq x_2\}$ is closed.
\item For all $x_1,x_2\in X$ with $x_1\not\leq x_2$, there are open sets $U_1,U_2$ with $x_1 \in U_1$, $x_2\in U_2$ such that for all $u_1\in U_1,\;u_2\in U_2,\ u_1\not\leq u_2$.
\item For all $x_1,x_2\in X$ with $x_1 \not\leq x_2$, there are disjoint neighbourhoods $U_1$ of $x_1$ and $U_2$ of $x_2$ such that $U_1$ is an upper set and $U_2$ is a lower set.
\end{DoxyItemize}

We say $\tau$ is an {\itshape order topology} for the partially-\/ordered set $(X,\leq)$.

In particular, for all $a\in X$, the sets $\{x\in X \mid x\leq a\}$ and $\{x\in X\mid x\geq a\}$ are closed, so the sets $\{x\in X \mid x\not\leq a\}$ and $\{x\in X\mid x\not\geq a\}$ are open. However, taking these as a sub-\/base for all open sets yields a topology that may be too coarse for an order topology.

{\itshape Example} We now give an example to show that the lower and upper sets, when taken as closed sets, need not generate an order topology. ~\newline
 Consider $\R\times\R$ with the standard product order $(x_1,y_1)\leq(x_2,y_2)\iff x_1\leq x_2\and y_1\leq y_2$. Suppose we take the sets $\{(x,y)\mid x\leq a\and y\leq b\}$ and $\{(x,y)\mid a\leq x\and b\leq y\}$ to generate the closed sets of the topology, so a sub-\/base of open sets is given by $\{(x,y)\mid x < a \or y < b\}$ and $\{(x,y)\mid a < x \or b < y\}$. Then any open set $U$ must contain a quadrants of the form $x< a \and y>b$ and $x>a \and y< b$, so any two open sets intersect, so the topology cannot yield a partially-\/ordered space. Note that in this case, the sets $\{ (x,y)  \mid \underline{a} < x < \overline{a} \and \underline{b} < y < \overline{b}\}$ are not open.\hypertarget{topology_page_partialorderedspace}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Partially ordered space}{Partially ordered space}}\label{topology_page_partialorderedspace}
Let $\prec$ be the way-\/ordered relation defined in section \doxysectlink{topology_page_robustpartialorder}{Robust verification of partial ordering}{3}, so $y\prec z$ if, and only if, whenever $Y$ is downward-\/directed and $Z$ is upward-\/directed with $\bigwedge Y\leq y$ and $z\leq \bigvee Z$, there exist $\tilde{y}\in Y$ and $\tilde{z}\in Z$ such that $\tilde{y}\leq \tilde{z}$.

{\itshape Definition} A set $U$ is open for the {\itshape lower order topology} if it is an upper-\/set (i.\+e. $x\in U$ whenever $u\leq x$ for some $u\in U$), and for all $u\in U$, there exists $v\in U$ with $v\prec u$. Similarly, a set $U$ is open for the {\itshape upper order topology} if it is a lower-\/set (i.\+e. $x\in U$ whenever $x\leq u$ for some $u\in U$), and for all $u\in U$, there exists $v\in U$ with $u\prec v$. The {\itshape order topology} is the common refinement of the lower-\/ and upper-\/ order toplogies.

{\itshape Theorem} The order topology gives $(X,\leq)$ the structure of a partially-\/ordered space.

If $U$ is open for the lower order topology, and $Z$ is an upward-\/directed set with $\bigvee Z\in U$, then $Z\cap U\neq \emptyset$. Similarly, if If $U$ is open for the upper order topology, and $Y$ is an downward-\/directed set with $\bigwedge Y\in U$, then $Y\cap U\neq \emptyset$.

We conjecture that if $U$ is an upper-\/set such that $\bigvee Z\in U \implies Z\cap U \neq \emptyset$ for all upward-\/directed $Z$, then $U$ is open for the lower topology.

{\itshape Theorem} Let $Q$ be a countable partially-\/ordered set, $X=\overline{Q}$, and suppose $Q$ is approximating in $X$. Then the sets $\{ x\in X \mid a \prec x \}$ for $a\in Q$ form a base for the lower order topology, and the sets $\{ x\in X \mid x \prec b \}$ for $b\in Q$ form a base for the upper order topology. The sets $\{ x\in X \mid a \prec x \prec b \}$ form a base for the order topology.\hypertarget{topology_page_lowerupperspaces}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Lower and upper spaces}{Lower and upper spaces}}\label{topology_page_lowerupperspaces}
The {\itshape lower reals} $\R_<$ comprises the set of real numbers, with topology given by intervals $(a\!:\!\infty)$ for $a\in\R$. The lower reals are not a Hausdorff space. In particular, the closed sets are the intervals $(-\infty\!:\!a]$ for $a\in\R$, while the saturated compact sets are intervals $[a\!:\!\infty)$ for $a\in\R$.\hypertarget{topology_page_scott_domain_section}{}\doxysubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Scott Domain Theory}{Scott Domain Theory}}\label{topology_page_scott_domain_section}
Domain theory was developed to provide a semantics of programming languages which could handle partially-\/defined recursive/iterative functions. It is based around complete lattices, with the main interpretation being the lattice of (open) sets of some space. However, the main notions are useful in computable analysis, and the definition of the "{}way-\/ordered"{} relation was motivated by the standard "{}way-\/below"{} relation of continuous Scott domains.\hypertarget{topology_page_directedcompletepartialorder}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Directed complete partial orders}{Directed complete partial orders}}\label{topology_page_directedcompletepartialorder}
A partially-\/ordered set $(X,\leq)$ is a {\itshape (countably) upward directed-\/complete partial order (dcpo)} if each of its (countable) upward directed subsets has a supremum. ~\newline
 In the literature, the "{}upward"{} is typically omitted, and one speaks of a "{}directed set"{} and "{}directed complete partial order"{}.

Note that a set is a countably upward directed-\/complete partial order if, and only if, every increasing sequence $x_0\leq x_1\leq x_2\leq\cdots$ has a supremum.

Clearly, any complete lattice is a directed complete partial order.

Suppose we want to verify $a\leq y$, where $a\in X$ is specified exactly, but $y$ is given as an increasing sequence $(y_n)_{n\in\N}$ with $y=\bigvee_{n=0}^{\infty}y_n$. Then we need that $a\leq y_n$ for some $n$. This motivates the following definition\+:

{\itshape Definition} Let $X$ be a complete lattice. Then the {\itshape (ω-\/)way-\/below} relation $\precless$ is defined by $a\precless y$ if whenever $Y$ is an upward-\/directed (countable) set with $y\leq\bigvee Y$, there exists $\tilde{y}\in Y$ with $a\leq\tilde{y}$.

Note that the way-\/below relation is usually denoted $\ll$ in the literature, but since $x\ll y$ is different from $y\gg x$, we instead use the symbol $\precless$.

It can easily be shown that the way-\/below relation is an {\itshape (lower) auxiliary order} satisfying\+:
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item $y\prec z\implies y\leq z$;
\item $u\leq y\prec z\leq v\implies u \prec v$;
\item $y_1\prec z\wedge y_2\prec z \implies y_1\vee y_2\prec z$.
\end{DoxyItemize}

The directed-\/complete partial order $X$ is {\itshape continuous} if for every $x \in X$, the set $\twoheaddownarrow  x:=\{a\in X\mid a\ll x\}$ is directed and has $x$ as its join. A {\itshape continuous domain} is a continuous directed-\/complete partial order. A {\itshape basis} of a continuous domain $X$ is a set $B$ such that for every $x \in X$, the set $(\twoheaddownarrow{x}) \cap B$ is directed and has $x$ as its join.\hypertarget{topology_page_scotttopology}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{The Scott topology}{The Scott topology}}\label{topology_page_scotttopology}
The {\itshape (ω-\/)Scott topology} on $X$ has open sets $U$ which are upper-\/sets such that if $Z$ is any (countable) upward-\/directed set with $\bigvee\!Z\,\in U$, then $Z\cap U\neq \emptyset$.

The Scott topology is the topology of lower semicontinuity.

If $X$ is the completion of a countable set (or more generally, if $X$ has a countable basis), then the Scott and ω-\/\+Scott topologies coincide\+: ~\newline
 {\itshape Theorem} Suppose $X=\overline{Q}$ for some countable partially-\/ordered set $Q$. Then $U$ is Scott-\/open if, and only if, it is ω-\/\+Scott-\/open. ~\newline
 {\itshape Proof} Note that trivially any Scott-\/open set is ω-\/\+Scott-\/open. Conversely, if $U$ is ω-\/\+Scott-\/open and $S$ is an upward-\/directed set with $\bigvee S\in U$. For any $s\in S$, the completion property gives $s=\bigvee(\downarrow\!\{s\}\cap Q)$. We claim $\bigvee S=\bigvee(\downarrow S\cap Q)$. Clearly $\bigvee(\downarrow S\cap Q)\leq \bigvee(\downarrow S)\leq \bigvee S$. Conversely for any $s\in S$, we have $s=\bigvee(\downarrow\{s\}\cap Q)\leq\bigvee(\downarrow S\cap Q)$, so $\bigvee S\leq \bigvee(\downarrow S\cap Q)$. Since $\downarrow S\cap Q$ is countable, $\exists x\in \downarrow S \cap Q$ such that $x\in U$. Since $x\in\downarrow S$, $\exists s\in S,\,x\leq s$, and so $s\in U$.\hypertarget{topology_page_lawsontopology}{}\doxysubsubsubsection{\texorpdfstring{The Lawson topology}{The Lawson topology}}\label{topology_page_lawsontopology}
The {\itshape dual topology} on a continuous domain is generated by the closed subbase consisting of sets of the form $\uparrow\!{a}:=\{x\in X \mid a \leq x\}$. ~\newline
 The dual topology is a topology of upper-\/semicontinuity.

The {\itshape Lawson topology} on $X$ is the smallest common refinement of the dual topology and the Scott topology on $X$.

{\bfseries{Examples}}

{\itshape The real numbers} Consider the rationals $\Q$ with the standard order. Then the lattice completion is the real numbers $\R$. Since the order $\leq$ is total, all sets are upper-\/ and lower-\/directed. The upper-\/sets are intervals $[a\!:\!\infty)$ and $(a\!:\!\infty)$ for $a\in\R$. The Scott topology has open sets $(a\:\infty)$ for $a\in\R$. The lower topology has closed sets $[a\!:\!\infty$, and open sets $(-\infty\!:a)$.

{\itshape Euclidean space} Consider the set of pairs of rationals $\Q^2$ with the product order $(p_1,q_1)\leq(p_2,q_2)\iff p_1\leq p_2\wedge q_1\leq q_2$. Then the lattice completion is the set of pairs of real numbers $\R^2$. The join is given by $(p_1,q_1)\vee(p_2,q_2)=(\max(p_1,p_2),\max(q_1,q_2))$. A basis for the Scott topology is given by sets of the form $\twoheaduparrow(a_1,a_2)=\{(x_1,x_2)\mid a_1 < x_1 \wedge a_2 < x_2\}=(a_1\!:\!\infty)\times(a_2\!:\!\infty)$. The lower topology is generated by has closed sets of the form $[a_1\!:\!\infty)\times[a_2\!:\!\infty)$, whose complements can be written $(-\infty\!:\!a_1)\times \R\cup\R\times(-\infty\!:\!a_2)$. Similarly, the upper topology is generated by has closed sets of the form $(-infty\!:\!a_1]\times(-\infty\!:\!a_2)$, whose complements can be written $(a_1\!:\!\infty)\times \R\cup\R\times(a_2\!:\!\infty)$.

This example shows that the upper topology is coarser than the the Scott toplogy. It turns out that for $\R^2$, the Lawson topology is equivalent to the Euclidean topology, but the lower and upper topologies do not suffice to generate the Euclidean topology.

For this reason, we sometimes call the Scott topology the {\itshape lower-\/\+Scott} topology, and its order-\/dual the {\itshape upper-\/\+Scott} topology.
